About

Daniel Palacios is an applied AI research scientist and PhD candidate in Quantitative & Computational Biosciences at Baylor College of Medicine, advised by Dr. Zhandong Liu in the Liu Lab at the Data Science Center, Texas Children’s Hospital. He builds AI systems that turn messy, real-world data into answers people can act on, owning the full pipeline from raw records to validated results.

His research spans four areas:

Clinical outcome prediction. He designs AI agents and LLM systems that predict patient outcomes and explain their reasoning through interpretable reports, with applications in postpartum depression risk and pediatric diabetes complications. These systems are benchmarked against classical machine learning and deep learning models.

Entity extraction. He uses LLMs, from agentic workflows to hybrid rule-and-model pipelines, to pull structure from clinical text, ranging from treatment-response phenotypes in rare disease research to PHI scrubbing for clinical-note de-identification.

Foundation models. He trains transformer-based models on electronic health records, including TEDDY, a generative pediatric EHR foundation model trained on data from millions of children.

End-to-end data analysis. He takes raw data to ML-ready through data curation, biomedical statistics (risk factors, odds and hazard ratios, clustering), and large-scale PySpark pipelines over standardized EHR data (OMOP CDM, Epic Clarity) on Palantir Foundry and AWS, so analyses are rigorous before any model is trained.

Daniel is also an AI Developer and Research Design Intern at Peraton, where he builds LLM and multi-agent software and designs controlled experiments to test how system design choices affect performance and reliability.

His path to AI has run through a wide range of scientific fields. As an undergraduate in physics and mathematics at the University of Houston, he modeled gene-regulatory dynamics and cell-fate decisions in bacteriophage lambda at Rice University’s Center for Theoretical Biological Physics, simulated quark-gluon plasma in heavy-ion collisions and validated the results against CERN ALICE data, and developed theory on asymmetric superconductivity that was published in Physical Review B. At NASA Johnson Space Center, he applied NLP to engineering failure reports to build a taxonomy for space-hardware failure analysis, and at NASA Ames Research Center, he built an agent-based model of radiation damage in microbial cells, validated against BioSentinel mission data. Across biophysics, particle physics, condensed matter theory, aerospace engineering, and clinical informatics, he has repeatedly stepped into unfamiliar domains, learned their problems quickly, and delivered validated results, an adaptability that now shapes how he approaches AI research applications.

An NSF Graduate Research Fellow and NLM Fellow, Daniel has received Baylor’s Dean’s Award of Excellence and an Outstanding TA Award. He has shared his work through peer-reviewed publications and more than 20 conference presentations.
